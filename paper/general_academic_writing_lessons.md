# General Lessons for Clear Academic Writing

## Purpose

This document records transferable lessons from repeated academic-writing
mistakes. It is intentionally independent of any particular paper, research
area, experiment, or dataset. Its purpose is to improve the logic, precision,
and readability of future research writing.

## Central lesson

Good academic writing does not begin by compressing all available information
into sentences. It begins by identifying the argument the reader must
understand. Evidence, qualifications, terminology, and transitions should then
support that argument in the correct logical order.

More information does not automatically produce a better sentence. In fact,
trying to preserve every detail at once often obscures the central claim. The
writer must decide which idea the sentence is responsible for expressing and
move secondary details to the appropriate supporting sentence, paragraph,
table, or appendix.

## Recurring mistakes and corrective rules

### 1. Drafting from an inventory instead of an argument

**Mistake:** Starting with all known facts, methods, variables, and caveats,
then attempting to compress them into prose. This produces sentences that are
technically dense but intellectually weak because the reader cannot identify
the point.

**Rule:** State the intended claim first. Add only the evidence needed to
establish that claim, followed by its consequence for the paper's question.

A useful paragraph structure is:

1. Claim: what the paragraph establishes.
2. Evidence: which observation or analysis establishes it.
3. Consequence: why it matters to the research question.

### 2. Reversing or obscuring causality

**Mistake:** Presenting an observed outcome as though it were the cause, or
giving causes, thresholds, and outcomes the same grammatical role.

**Rule:** Identify the causal chain before writing the sentence:

1. What system behavior was observed?
2. Which factor changed that behavior?
3. What criterion determines whether the behavior is acceptable?
4. What application or scientific consequence follows?

The grammar must preserve this direction. The cause should act on the measured
outcome, and the outcome should be compared with the acceptance criterion.

### 3. Combining capability, limitation, and consequence into one vague claim

**Mistake:** Mixing what a system can do, the conditions that affect it, and
whether the result satisfies a requirement without showing how these ideas are
related.

**Rule:** Separate the layers explicitly:

- Capability: what the system can accomplish.
- Limiting factors: what changes its performance.
- Criterion: how success is judged.
- Consequence: when the system can support the intended use.

A reusable form is:

> The system can perform **X**, but **Y** and **Z** determine whether the result
> satisfies **criterion C**.

This structure is preferable to saying that unrelated concepts must somehow
"fit" one another.

### 4. Giving one sentence too many logical jobs

**Mistake:** Requiring one sentence to introduce a capability, enumerate all
experimental variables, qualify the scope, report a result, and state an
implication.

**Rule:** Give each sentence one primary logical job. A sentence may contain a
contrast or transition when the two clauses form one clear relationship.
Otherwise, split the reasoning into short sentences and order them so that each
sentence creates the need for the next.

Long sentences are justified only when separating their clauses would break a
single, necessary comparison, contrast, or causal relationship.

### 5. Choosing academic-sounding words instead of accurate verbs

**Mistake:** Using broad or polished-sounding verbs because they resemble
research prose, even when they do not describe the technical relationship.
Vague verbs often hide who acts, what changes, or how success is decided.

**Rule:** Choose the verb only after identifying the relationship:

- Use **causes**, **increases**, **reduces**, or **determines** for a supported
  causal relationship.
- Use **is associated with**, **varies with**, or **corresponds to** when the
  evidence is correlational.
- Use **exceeds**, **falls below**, or **satisfies** for comparison with a
  threshold.
- Use **supports**, **carries**, or **completes** only after defining the object,
  endpoint, and success criterion.

Never keep a word merely because it sounds concise. If the subjects and objects
do not participate in the relationship expressed by the verb, rewrite the
sentence.

### 6. Allowing qualifications to overwhelm the insight

**Mistake:** Putting every boundary, caveat, variable, and exception into a
headline claim. The result reads as defensive commentary rather than an
insight.

**Rule:** State the strongest claim directly supported by the evidence. Put the
measurement scope and necessary qualifications in the supporting paragraph.
The main claim should remain understandable when read by itself, but it should
not imply a broader result than the evidence establishes.

Scope should be expressed through precise subjects and conditions, not through
repeated disclaimers.

### 7. Listing related information without synthesizing it

**Mistake:** Naming standards, systems, methods, experiments, or results one
after another without explaining their collective meaning.

**Rule:** Every group of facts must answer three questions:

1. What common role do these facts play?
2. What pattern, contrast, or limitation do they establish together?
3. How does that conclusion advance the paper's argument?

If the paragraph cannot answer these questions, it is an inventory rather than
an argument. A list is appropriate only when the items are genuinely parallel
and the surrounding prose interprets the list.

### 8. Treating transitions as word choices instead of logical relationships

**Mistake:** Selecting a transition because two sentences discuss different
topics, because the prose appears to need a connector, or because the word
sounds academic. This error labels parallel facts as a contrast, an additional
fact as a consequence, or a change of subject as a qualification. Replacing the
connector with a different transition word does not repair the reasoning.

Common forms of this mistake include:

1. using **in contrast** or **whereas** merely because the second sentence
   introduces a different application, technology, or example;
2. using **therefore**, **thus**, or **consequently** when the preceding
   sentence does not establish a cause or premise;
3. using **however** or **nevertheless** when the second statement does not
   contradict, limit, or violate an expectation created by the first;
4. using **moreover** or **in addition** when the new fact does not extend the
   same claim; and
5. using **first**, **next**, or **finally** when the order is editorial rather
   than procedural, temporal, or argumentative.

**Rule:** Determine the relationship between the complete ideas before choosing
any transition. Write the two propositions in plain language and test the
proposed relationship:

1. **Cause:** Does the first proposition produce or explain the second?
2. **Consequence:** Does the second proposition logically follow from the
   first?
3. **Contrast:** Are both propositions comparable on the same dimension, and
   do they differ on that dimension?
4. **Qualification:** Does the second proposition narrow a claim or violate an
   expectation created by the first?
5. **Extension:** Does the second proposition add evidence or scope to the same
   claim?
6. **Sequence:** Must one event, method, or reasoning step occur before the
   other?

If the relationship cannot pass one of these tests, do not insert a connector.
Instead, repair the reasoning by adding the missing premise, reordering the
ideas, splitting a sentence, or deleting the unrelated statement.

A strong transition often does not require a conjunctive adverb. Repeating the
technical subject or using a precise backward-pointing phrase can establish the
relationship more clearly. Phrases such as **this coverage limit**, **these
measurements**, **the same workload**, or **the preceding comparison** tell the
reader exactly which idea continues into the next sentence. They are preferable
to a generic connector when the relationship is continuation, evidence, or
interpretation rather than contrast or causation.

The same rule applies across paragraphs and sections. The final sentence of one
unit must establish the conclusion, limitation, or unresolved question that the
next unit takes up. A heading or a transition word cannot substitute for that
logical handoff.

When revising a transition error, use this procedure:

1. remove the transition word and state both ideas independently;
2. identify the exact relationship, if one exists;
3. add any missing premise or reorder the ideas;
4. choose a connector or backward-pointing phrase that names the true
   relationship; and
5. reread the boundary in both directions to confirm that the second idea
   follows from the first and prepares the next one.

### 9. Writing commentary instead of scholarship

**Mistake:** Explaining what the authors are not claiming, anticipating an
invisible reviewer, describing the writing process, or inserting backstage
notes into prose, captions, tables, or figures.

**Rule:** Address concerns through evidence, experimental design, precise scope,
and appropriately bounded claims. The manuscript should present the scientific
argument directly. It should not narrate how the authors expect a reviewer to
react.

Captions should identify what is shown, how values are defined, and what
comparison the reader can make. They should not direct the reader to omitted
detail or explain editorial decisions.

### 10. Repeating information because each section was drafted independently

**Mistake:** Restating background, methods, results, or insights in several
sections at nearly the same level of detail.

**Rule:** Assign a distinct role to every section:

- The abstract presents the problem, gap, contributions, principal result, and
  insights at the highest level.
- The introduction establishes importance, the central question, and the
  contribution logic.
- Related work synthesizes prior knowledge and identifies the unresolved gap.
- Design and methods explain what was built or measured and why the evaluation
  answers the question.
- Results present evidence and comparisons.
- Discussion interprets the combined evidence.
- The conclusion states the resolved argument and contribution without
  repeating the paper as a list.

When an idea must recur, change its function and level of detail. A brief
preview, a full analysis, and a final implication are not repetition when each
serves a different purpose.

### 11. Letting terminology drift

**Mistake:** Alternating between near-synonyms, assistant-created labels, or
undefined abbreviations. Readers may interpret each variation as a different
technical concept.

**Rule:** Use one stable term for one concept. Define specialized terminology
and spell out abbreviations at first use. Prefer familiar words unless a
technical term adds necessary precision. When two terms are intentionally
different, define the distinction before relying on it.

### 12. Treating visual elements as storage rather than arguments

**Mistake:** Using figures and tables to hold excess information without a clear
comparison, scale, unit, threshold, or takeaway.

**Rule:** Every visual should make a relationship easier to understand than
prose would. A reader should be able to determine:

1. what is being compared;
2. the units and denominator;
3. which direction is better or worse;
4. the relevant threshold or reference point; and
5. the conclusion supported by the visual.

Remove panels that do not advance the argument. Combine panels only when their
shared visual structure makes the comparison clearer.

Choose the visual form from the comparison the reader must make. Use a line or
point plot for an ordered sweep when the trend across payload, signal, demand,
or time matters. Use a heatmap when the reader must inspect a dense, complete
two-dimensional matrix. Use actual units when a normalized ratio would force
the reader to reconstruct the numerator, denominator, or acceptance direction.
Place the relevant deadline or threshold on the same axis as the measurement.
Every cell, marker, axis, and reference line must expose its unit or denominator
at final publication size.

### 13. Confusing the history of a field with the research gap

**Mistake:** Describing how standards, systems, or research directions were
developed and then treating that history as the missing capability. Work may
have developed separately without creating an important gap, and independently
developed components may already form a coherent whole. Historical separation
is therefore context, not proof of what the field presently lacks.

**Rule:** Define the research gap as a present missing capability, untested
relationship, unresolved question, or unmet requirement. Use development
history only when evidence shows that it explains the present limitation.

A gap paragraph should follow this logic:

1. establish what prior work collectively provides;
2. name the precise capability or evidence that is still absent;
3. explain why that absence matters to the research question; and
4. introduce the contribution by showing how it supplies the same capability
   or evidence just identified.

The scope of an absence claim must also match the intended novelty claim. “The
reviewed studies do not provide X” is a claim about a selected body of work.
“The field does not provide X” is a broader claim about the current state of the
area. Use the broader form only when the literature search and definition of X
support it, but do not accidentally weaken a domain-level contribution by
describing it as an artifact of the surveyed sample.

The transition from gap to contribution should repeat the missing object rather
than introduce a new description. If the gap is the absence of X, the next
sentence should state directly how the contribution provides X. Mere adjacency
does not establish that the proposed work solves the stated problem.

### 14. Comparing categories at different levels of abstraction

**Mistake:** Defining two conceptual classes but naming one class through its
implementation object and the other through its application behavior. For
example, a sentence may compare a message format with a stateful operation even
though the intended claim concerns two classes of applications. The sentence
may appear grammatically balanced while remaining conceptually asymmetric.

**Rule:** Compare concepts at the same level of abstraction. If the claim is
about application classes, name both as application classes. If the claim is
about interaction patterns, name both patterns. If the claim is about encoded
objects, name both object types. Explain the mapping between levels in a
separate sentence.

Before writing a comparison, place each term in its hierarchy:

1. system or protocol;
2. application class;
3. interaction pattern;
4. application object; and
5. encoded representation.

Choose the level required by the claim and keep both sides at that level. A
clear explanation can then move downward explicitly: the protocol supports two
application classes; one class uses independent messages, while the other uses
correlated operations. This sequence preserves both conceptual symmetry and
implementation detail.

### 15. Using qualitative labels without quantitative definitions

**Mistake:** Describing measured conditions with informal labels such as good,
favorable, typical, or weak without defining their numeric boundaries. The
same observation may then receive different labels in prose, tables, and
figures, or a legacy experiment name may contradict the classification used in
the analysis.

**Rule:** Define one quantitative classification before interpreting the
results. State the metric, unit, inclusive boundaries, and measurement source.
Apply those boundaries mechanically to every observation, and replace legacy
labels throughout the prose, tables, figures, captions, and legends. If no
observation falls in a defined category, say so instead of assigning the
nearest condition to it.

Classification and causality remain separate. A threshold can place an
observation in a category, but it does not prove that the classified variable
caused the measured outcome. When another factor changed with the category,
present the result as a joint-condition comparison.

### 16. Presenting results in acquisition order instead of explanatory order

**Mistake:** Reporting experiments in the order they were collected or found
in the result archive. Readers then encounter compound conditions before the
single-variable baseline, move backward from large workloads to small ones, or
see a figure several pages after the paragraph that interprets it.

**Rule:** Order results by explanatory complexity:

1. validate the representation or measurement;
2. establish the single-path and single-flow baseline;
3. vary one workload or protocol factor;
4. introduce field or network conditions;
5. introduce competing demand and concurrency; and
6. evaluate interruption, recovery, and combined implications.

Every table and figure must be cited and interpreted in the surrounding text.
Place it on the same page as the principal interpretation whenever layout
permits. Plot labels must use reader-facing application and measurement terms,
not internal run names. Spell out sample counts, units, denominators, and
completion criteria directly in the visual or caption. Numerical prose must
reproduce the corresponding table values and ranges exactly.

### 17. Giving a table no distinct analytical purpose

**Mistake:** Repeating a system diagram in tabular form or combining equipment,
dates, workloads, and results simply because the information is available. The
reader cannot tell what comparison the table enables, and the surrounding prose
has no conclusion to draw from it.

**Rule:** Give every table one analytical purpose. State that purpose before the
table, select only columns that serve it, and explain the comparison afterward.
For an experimental-control table, map each controlled condition to the
interpretive role it enables. Put acquisition dates in provenance records unless
time is itself the comparison variable.

### 18. Naming application categories without naming their communication

**Mistake:** Describing a broad application label without identifying the actor
that sends each message, the direction of the exchange, or the returned object.
The label then hides the workload that the experiment is supposed to represent.

**Rule:** When an application motivates a communication experiment, name the
concrete interaction. Identify the sender, receiver, request or update, response,
direction, and timing requirement. If a standardized message pair represents
the interaction, use its established names and verify their current roles.

### 19. Organizing evidence by collection history instead of comparison logic

**Mistake:** Grouping sample counts or experiments by day even though the paper
compares protocol, direction, payload, signal, device, or configuration. This
makes chronology appear analytically important and fragments matched evidence.

**Rule:** Aggregate and order evidence by the variable that answers the research
question. Report the denominator once at the level used by the analysis. Use a
short numbered structure when several experiment families have parallel roles;
do not bury those roles in a long chronological paragraph.

### 20. Drawing continuity through unmeasured conditions

**Mistake:** Connecting sparse measurements with a line, merging protocols with
different evaluated payloads, or filling an unmeasured cell with a neighboring
value. The visual implies evidence that the experiment did not collect.

**Rule:** Preserve the exact measurement grid. Show unmeasured combinations as
explicit gaps or neutral cells, keep distinct transports or configurations
separate, and state the denominator in the caption. Validate the visual at its
final column width so that units, legends, thresholds, and gap symbols remain
readable.

### 21. Reporting a replication without stating what it tests

**Mistake:** Introducing a second device or repeated configuration as another
result block without explaining which earlier uncertainty it addresses. The
reader cannot distinguish replication from an unrelated experiment.

**Rule:** State the uncertainty before the replication. Then identify what is
held constant, what changes, and which outcome would support or reject the
suspected explanation. Present the replication next to the original comparison,
and place its figure before the synthesis that depends on it.

### 22. Treating the collection method as the limiting factor

**Mistake:** Naming a result after how the data were collected and then implying
that the collection method caused the outcome. A route collected from a moving
vehicle may contain weak or absent coverage, but motion is not established as
the cause unless speed or motion was varied while the radio path was otherwise
controlled.

**Rule:** Name the factor that the evidence actually identifies. Separate the
collection procedure, the observed operating condition, and the candidate
cause. Use spatial or route-coverage language when failures recur at particular
locations. Use mobility, speed, handover, or Doppler as explanations only when
the experiment measures or controls those mechanisms. Figures and subsection
titles must follow the same distinction.

## A reusable revision procedure

### Pass 1: Reconstruct the argument

Write the paper's problem, gap, contributions, main result, and implications in
plain language. Do not edit sentences until these elements form one coherent
chain.

### Pass 2: Audit causal direction

For every result and insight, label the observation, cause or associated
factor, criterion, and consequence. Rewrite any sentence whose grammar changes
that order.

### Pass 3: Audit sentence responsibilities

State the primary job of each sentence in the margin: claim, evidence,
definition, comparison, qualification, consequence, or transition. Split
sentences with several unrelated jobs and remove sentences with no necessary
job.

### Pass 4: Audit paragraph synthesis

For each paragraph, write one sentence describing what the paragraph proves.
If the paragraph only reports what exists or what was done, add the missing
pattern and consequence or move the material to a more appropriate location.

### Pass 5: Audit transitions

Check every adjacent sentence, paragraph boundary, and section boundary. Name
the actual relationship. Do not begin by replacing one connector with another.
First verify that the two propositions have the claimed causal, contrastive,
qualifying, additive, evidentiary, or sequential relationship. Add a precise
transition only when it truthfully expresses that relationship. When a
backward-pointing technical phrase makes the connection clearer, use it instead
of a generic conjunctive adverb.

### Pass 6: Audit word choice and terminology

Underline every technical noun and every causal verb. Confirm that each term is
defined, each verb matches the evidence, and no synonym creates an unintended
distinction.

### Pass 7: Audit evidence strength

Ask whether every strong verb and general conclusion is supported by the
experiment or analysis. Strengthen underclaimed conclusions and narrow
overclaimed ones. Do not replace this audit with generic hedging.

### Pass 8: Read as an informed outsider

Read the manuscript without relying on unstated project knowledge. Every
sentence should make clear what its subject is, what happened, why it happened,
how success was judged, and why the result matters. If the reader must infer one
of these elements, revise the sentence or its surrounding context.

## Final writing checklist

- Does the paper begin from a coherent argument rather than an inventory of
  information?
- Does every causal sentence preserve the direction from factor to outcome to
  criterion?
- Are capability, limitation, success criterion, and consequence clearly
  distinguished?
- Does each sentence have one primary logical job?
- Do verbs describe the actual relationship supported by the evidence?
- Are qualifications placed in supporting prose instead of overwhelming the
  main insight?
- Does every paragraph synthesize its evidence into a conclusion?
- Is each research gap a present missing capability or unanswered question,
  rather than a historical description of how prior work developed?
- Does the contribution explicitly provide the same capability or evidence
  named in the gap?
- Are both sides of every comparison expressed at the same level of
  abstraction?
- When application classes map to different messages, operations, or encoded
  objects, is that mapping explained separately from the class-level claim?
- Can the relationship expressed by every transition be stated and defended in
  plain language?
- Do paragraph and section openings identify the specific prior conclusion,
  limitation, or question they continue?
- Has commentary and anticipated-reviewer dialogue been replaced by evidence
  and precise scope?
- Does each section perform a distinct role without repeating earlier detail?
- Is one stable term used for each concept?
- Can every figure and table be understood through its comparison, units,
  denominator, threshold, and supported conclusion?
- Does each visual encoding match the comparison: trend, matrix, threshold, or
  distribution?
- Are collection procedures kept separate from the factors claimed to explain
  the result?
- Is every qualitative condition defined by a metric, unit, and nonoverlapping
  numeric range, and is that classification consistent across text and visuals?
- Do numerical sentences reproduce the values and ranges in the referenced
  table without changing the denominator or comparison?
- Do the results progress from baselines and single-variable comparisons to
  compound conditions, concurrent demand, and failures?
- Is every figure and table cited and placed beside the prose that performs its
  principal interpretation?
- Can an informed reader outside the immediate specialty follow the argument
  without reconstructing missing logic?
